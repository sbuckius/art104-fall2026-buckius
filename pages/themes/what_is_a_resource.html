General ideas and terms9 terms
Term 1

Natural resource

Anything from nature that people use: water, soil, air, sunlight, plants, animals, minerals, fuels.

Read more
Term 2

Renewable and nonrenewable

Renewable resources refill on a human timescale, like sun, wind and forests if cared for. Nonrenewable ones, like oil, lithium and sand, take millions of years to form.

National Geographic
Term 3

Land, labor, capital

Economics calls these the factors of production: the earth, people's work, and the tools and money used to make things.

Read more
Term 4

Human resources

The name for workers inside a company, and for the department that manages them. People counted alongside materials.

Read more
Term 5

Critical minerals

Minerals a government decides its economy and security depend on, like lithium, cobalt and rare earths. The list changes as needs change.

The US list
Term 6

Extractivism

An economy built on removing large amounts of raw material, usually shipped elsewhere, with the damage left where it was dug.

Read more
Term 7

Resource curse

The pattern where places rich in oil or minerals often end up poorer, less stable or less democratic than places without them.

Read more
Term 8

The commons

Resources shared by a community, like a fishery or a pasture. Some argued they always get overused; Elinor Ostrom showed many communities manage them well for centuries.

Elinor Ostrom
Term 9

Ecosystem services

The work nature does for free: bees pollinating, wetlands filtering water, forests storing carbon. Naming it this way puts nature's care in economic terms.

Read more

The word itself

A spring that rises again

"Resource" comes from the Latin resurgere, to rise again, through the French for a spring. The meaning of a country's wealth only arrived around 1779.

Erich Zimmermann · 1933

Resources are not, they become

Nothing is a resource on its own. Earth's stuff becomes one only when people decide it is useful, so what counts changes over time.

Robin Wall Kimmerer · Potawatomi

It or ki?

English calls a tree, a river or a robin "it." Potawatomi speaks of them as living beings. Calling nature an object makes it easier to treat as property. Kimmerer offers "ki," plural "kin."
