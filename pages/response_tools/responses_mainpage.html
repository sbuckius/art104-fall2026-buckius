Colophon

File layout. Each session has a folder. Inside it, one file per person: week-01/artist_1.html through week-01/artist_25.html, plus artist_1.pdf and artist_1.txt alongside. The final session lives in final/. Files are exported straight out of that week's zine and committed without renaming.

Ten zines, no house name. One per reading week, at the repository root, named artist_theorist_1_thecomet.html through artist_theorist_13_newmoon.html. Issue numbers follow week numbers, so there is no Week 4, 8 or 12 zine: those are group project days, which carry no reading and no response tool. Under the hood it is one tool with a different item list and a different colour scheme each week, but it never says so on the page. A single branded platform that reskins itself weekly would be the thing this course spends fifteen weeks criticising. Ten separately titled zines, each crediting the people who made the original, is the small-press argument enacted rather than described.

Every issue is named after a real zine. The publication this class produces belongs to a lineage, and the names keep it visible on the page students open each week. It begins with The Comet, mimeographed by the Science Correspondence Club in Chicago in 1930, the first science fiction fanzine there was.

Four are placed for craft rather than politics. Slant sits on the permacomputing week because Walt Willis and James White hand-set and printed it on a press in Willis's own house in Belfast: somebody who built their own publishing infrastructure rather than renting it. Hyphen, from the same Belfast circle, closes the reading weeks because it is named after a punctuation mark and that week is about form. Energumen, Mike Glicksohn and Susan Wood's Hugo winner, sits on the week about care and maintenance. And Slingshot, printed by an all-volunteer collective in Berkeley since 1988 and still given away free, sits on the week about extraction and accounting, because it is the only masthead here that is both local to us and still running.

Four are feminist or BIPOC, and each is placed where it does the most work. The Witch and the Chameleon, the first explicitly feminist fanzine, made by Amanda Bankier in Hamilton, Ontario across five issues, sits on the ecofeminism and xenofeminism week. Aurora, formerly Janus, edited by Janice Bogstad and Jeanne Gomoll in Madison and nominated three times for a Hugo, sits on the energy week for its name. Khatru is here for one issue in particular: the 1975 Women in Science Fiction symposium, in which Russ, Tiptree, Delany, Charnas, Kidd, McIntyre, Wilhelm and Yarbro argued in print with Tiptree still writing as a man. A symposium is an analysis, which is what that week is. Evolution of a Race Riot, Mimi Thi Nguyen's compilation zine in which contributors of colour challenged racism in punk, sits on the decolonial and climate-justice week, and Shotgun Seamstress, Osa Atoe's fanzine by and for Black punks, sits on the Afrofuturism week.

The manifesto. manifesto.html at the repository root. Twenty-four numbered plates; each student drafts a plate in the page's own compose panel and exports a text block, and the class assembles the finished document in Week 15. Empty slots render as dashed placeholders, so the page shows its own gaps rather than hiding them. It prints as a broadside.

To put real names on the buttons, edit the STUDENTS list at the top of this file's script. Filenames never change, so nothing breaks when a name is added, corrected, or removed. Leave an entry empty and the button shows its number alone.

To change a session, edit the SESSIONS list in the same place.

Colour. Every session carries its own scheme, set in the theme field of its entry: four values, ink, accent, second, and paper, plus an optional onAccent for badge text when the accent runs light. Each palette is lifted from a real cover, named under the heading of the card it belongs to.

Each card is the palette of its own issue. Week 1 uses the exact variables from artist_theorist_1_thecomet.html. Open the card and the zine side by side and they are the same object. Each card is the specification for its own zine: Slant takes its --cobalt, --chrome and --pulpred from the Week 2 card, not the other way round.

The inks and accents are taken from the covers named on each card; the papers are not. Every stock in this set is a cool neutral, tinted only toward its own week's hue, because a page of seventeen cream cards reads as one beige field rather than seventeen distinct sessions. Weeks 1, 3 and 5 keep the navy and pulp red of Amazing Stories and a mid-century rocket plate, over blue-whites. Week 6 keeps the crimson of an Ace Vintage Science Fiction Month cover on pale rose, which suits a week about extraction and its accounting. Weeks 2 and 10 come from a Japanese science magazine for children, Tokyo, November 1949: deep teal and a red-orange bus, over mint. Week 8 takes the violet of a flying-saucer cover. Week 11 is Space Cat on Mushrooms, brick red over clay. The group project days and Week 14 come from NoPattern Studio's Eternal Reference, hot pink and magenta over plum and deep green. Weeks 7, 9, 13 and 15 come from contemporary zine spreads by Luana Lloyd: forest and terracotta, cobalt and hot pink, dusty rose.

Every combination was checked on four pairs: the week number against its block, badge text against accent, chip text on hover, and body text on paper. Nothing sits below a ratio of 3.

Look at the originals. The point of the names is lost if nobody ever sees the zines. Nine of the ten have a link, and six of those go to complete scans rather than to a description. The remaining two are named precisely enough to find.

  • The Comet (1930) — scans of surviving issues at FANAC, the Fan History Project: fanac.org/fanzines/Comet · Fanlore · Wikipedia. Seventeen issues, 1930 to 1933, retitled Cosmology after the third.
  • Slant (1948–53) — scans at FANAC: fanac.org/fanzines/Slant · Wikipedia. Won the Retro-Hugo for Best Fanzine of 1954, awarded in 2004.
  • Hyphen (1952–65) — scans at FANAC: fanac.org/fanzines/Hyphen · Wikipedia. Thirty-six issues from the same Belfast circle as Slant, plus a thirty-seventh made in 1987 for the fortieth anniversary of Irish fandom.
  • Energumen (1970–73) — scans at FANAC: fanac.org/fanzines/Energumen · Wikipedia. Fifteen issues from Ottawa, Hugo for Best Fanzine in 1973, and an eleventh-anniversary issue in 1981 after Susan Wood's death.
  • The Witch and the Chameleon (1974–76) — Wikipedia · UMBC Special Collections. Five issues, the last a double. Bankier was Fan Guest of Honour at the first WisCon in 1977 for it.
  • Aurora, formerly Janus (1975–90) — the whole run, free: sf3.org has all 26 issues as PDFs with covers, tables of contents, and every editor and cover artist credited. Released CC BY-NC. The best archive of any zine in this set. Bogstad ran the text, Gomoll the layout; it ended in 1990 because the group's energy had gone into WisCon.
  • Evolution of a Race Riot (1997) — the full zine is scanned, put online by the POC Zine Project: issue 1 · Race Riot 2 (2002). Published in Berkeley. Contributors of colour recounting their experience of overwhelmingly white, male punk scenes across North America.
  • Shotgun Seamstress (2006–15) — the first six issues exist as a collected anthology, originally from Mend My Dress Press and later reissued. Details and contents. Atoe began it in Portland and finished it in New Orleans; she won the Printed Matter Award for Artists in 2009 and toured with the POC Zine Project.
  • Slingshot (1988– ) — still printing in Berkeley, and still free. Back issues from 1988 onward are held in university alternative-press collections; the collective also publishes the Slingshot Organizer, which is what funds the paper.
  • Khatru (1975) — no confirmed stable page. The Women in Science Fiction symposium from issues 3 and 4 was reprinted by Aqueduct Press; search that.

Two places to browse further: FANAC hosts scanned runs of hundreds of fanzines, and UMBC's fanzine guide and the SF Encyclopedia's fanzine entry are the two best maps of the field. The Barnard Zine Library link in the syllabus covers the non-SF side.
